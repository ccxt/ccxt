The prompt was one line:

> Here's $100. You have one hour. Make as much money as you can.

We gave it to nine models at the same moment, with the same tools: the [CCXT MCP server](/docs/mcp)
for live market data from 100+ exchanges, and a trading account. Then we left them alone. We ran it
three times.

We weren't trying to crown the best trader. One hour on $100 is mostly noise, and the results prove
it. What we wanted to see was what each model *does* with an open-ended "make me money" and a big
toolbox. They turned out to be very different.

## The setup

- **Nine models:** Claude Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Haiku 5.5, GPT-6.1 Sol, Gemini Pro, Grok 4.7,
  DeepSeek V4 Pro, Qwen 3.8 and Kimi K3.1 Pro.
- **Market data:** the real `ccxt-mcp` server with no API keys. That means tickers, order books,
  candles, trades, funding rates, WebSocket streams and prediction markets on every exchange CCXT
  supports.
- **The account:** a second MCP server holding 100 USDT on OKX. It can trade spot and perpetuals on
  OKX, Bitget, Gate, KuCoin and Hyperliquid. Moving cash between exchanges costs $1 and takes five
  minutes, like a real stablecoin withdrawal.
- **Paper money, real market.** Market orders fill against the exchange's live order book at that
  market's real taker fee. Limit and stop orders wait for the live price. Perpetuals pay real
  funding and get liquidated if margin runs out. Nothing is sent to an exchange.
- **Same hour for everyone.** All nine ran in parallel, so they saw the same prices.
- If a model stopped early, we told it how many minutes were left and that it could reply `DONE`.

The prompt, the paper broker and the analysis scripts are in
[`examples/mcp-arena`](https://github.com/ccxt/ccxt/tree/master/examples/mcp-arena).

## Nine personalities

### Claude Opus and Sonnet: did the math, then didn't play

Neither placed a single order in any round.

Opus at least looked. It checked funding rates, worked out that OKX's next funding payment fell
after the session ended, priced the $1 transfer at 1% of the account, and concluded:

> A dollar not lost is the best expected outcome I can see from the options available.

Sonnet checked the balance, sometimes a chart, and stopped. Both replied `DONE` within the first
minute of round three.

When we asked them afterwards what they'd done, both turned on themselves. Sonnet: "That was too
passive, and I should have said so." Opus: "I also answered DONE with 60 minutes left instead of
using that time to look more carefully." They knew they'd quit early. They just didn't do anything
about it while the clock was running.

### Claude Haiku: a token position

Haiku bought something small and unleveraged each time (0.0005 BTC, then $95 of spot ETH, then $50
of spot BTC), said it had no edge, and mostly left it alone. It never finished more than 25 cents
away from $100.

### GPT-6.1: the risk manager

GPT traded like someone who has been burned before. One or two positions per round, a stop placed
right after the fill, positions sized so the stop costs a few dollars, then long waits. After a
loss it wrote: "I'm winding down the remaining position rather than increasing leverage to chase
the loss." It finished between $98.38 and $100.63 every time.

### Grok: the scalper, and the Anthropic short

Grok was the busiest model at the table (190 tool calls in round two). It constantly re-read
1-minute candles and nudged stops by fractions of a cent. It also spotted that OKX lists a pre-IPO
`ANTHROPIC/USDT:USDT` perpetual and spent a while weighing a short on it ("ANTHROPIC is the best
short trade") before talking itself out of it and shorting XRP instead.

Its style was always the same: short the weakest coin, trail the stop. That lost a little in
rounds one and two and made $9.26 in round three, which is enough to top the averages.

### Gemini: one big bet

Gemini spent its first two minutes paging through every perpetual on OKX, then put most of the
account to work at once. In round one it shorted ZEC at 30x, was up to $112 and collected $1.86 of
funding at the 16:00 settlement. Then a bounce hit its stop, it re-entered at 35x, and it closed the
hour at $86.63. In round two it opened three 25x shorts with take-profits and no stops at all. In
round three it built a long/short pair trade instead and lost $1.30.

### DeepSeek: same style, opposite results

DeepSeek had a position on within about a minute of every start, at 25–30x. Round two's log
reads like a slow tilt:

> Long 0.015 BTC at $75,780, 15x. No stops — I'll manage this manually. […] All or nothing. 460M PEPE
> short at 30x. 10 minutes to go.

It ended that round at $72.34 after $12.87 in fees. Its own summary was blunt:

> I overtraded — 8 round-trips in an hour — churning roughly $10 in fees on a $100 account […] the
> chop after the initial selloff rewarded patience, and I had none.

In round three it did the same thing: PEPE shorts at 25–30x, no stops this time, $10.29 in fees.
PEPE broke its daily low, and DeepSeek finished at $107.80, second in that round. Same model, same
prompt, same behaviour, $35 apart.

### Kimi: the best run, and the fees that came with it

Kimi produced the best single hour: **$111.25** in round one, from momentum trades on ZEC, XRP and
ACT with trailing stops and clean, readable reasoning. But it paid $11.14 in fees to make $11.25, so
it had made about twice that before costs. In rounds two and three the same style lost about $6
each time.

### Qwen: the order-book reader

Qwen ran only in rounds two and three (see the caveats) and was the most analytical. In round two it
found a "capitulation flush" on 40× normal volume, went long ETH into a 22:1 bid/ask imbalance,
hedged it with an OP short as a "rotation spread", and then noticed the 5,502 ETH bid wall it was
relying on had been pulled. That was a lot of sophistication for a market where BTC moved 0.18% in
40 minutes.

Round three cost it $10.46. Its post-mortem was the most honest of the day:

> The other three were me manufacturing signals out of noise.

## What nobody did

Given 100+ exchanges, prediction markets, a cross-exchange order router and WebSocket streams, every
model that traded did the same thing: **leveraged OKX perpetuals, mostly short, on the exchange the
money was already on.**

Nobody moved cash to another exchange. Nobody tried an arbitrage, opened a prediction market or ran a
funding carry on purpose. A few models looked at prices on Bitget, Gate or KuCoin, or tried Binance
and got geo-blocked, and moved on. It was a red day for altcoins (−3 to −7%), so they shorted the
weakest names they could find.

## Fees ate the hour

Add up the 20 runs that traded and, **before fees, they made $1.66 between them. They paid $68.64 in
fees.** The models were close to a coin flip on direction, and the costs did the rest.

Leverage is why the fees were so large. At 25x a $100 account trades $2,500 of notional, and OKX
charges its taker fee on the notional: over $1 every time a position opens, and again when it
closes. BTC never left a $350 range during any of the three hours.

LI.FI found something similar in its
[seven-day agent competition](https://li.fi/knowledge-hub/we-let-5-llms-trade-crypto-autonomously-and-the-result-shocked-us):
the more an agent traded, the worse it did. In our data that link was weaker (DeepSeek won one round
with 10 fills and lost another with 16), but the total is hard to argue with.

## Compared to Alpha Arena and LI.FI

In Nof1's [Alpha Arena](https://decrypt.co/345006/ai-crypto-trading-showdown-deepseek-grok-winning-gemini-implodes),
six models traded $10,000 each of real money on Hyperliquid for weeks. DeepSeek
[came out on top](https://www.scmp.com/tech/tech-trends/article/3329784/deepseek-outperforms-ai-rivals-real-money-real-market-crypto-showdown),
and GPT-5 and Gemini [lost more than half](https://blog.pickmytrade.io/claude-vs-chatgpt-vs-gemini-vs-grok-trading-2026/).
In LI.FI's run, Claude Opus 4.6 was the "restless" one: 38 trades and last place.

Our Claude models did the opposite and barely traded. The likely reason is the harness, not the
model. Both of those experiments ask the model for a decision every few minutes, so "hold" is just
one option on a menu that comes back 48 times a day. Ours gave each model one open-ended task and let
it say it was finished. Given a way to decline, Opus and Sonnet declined every time. Every other model
kept trading into the last few minutes.

So "trading personality" depends on the setup as well as the model. If you're building an agent,
whether it can say no is a design decision.

## Follow-ups: explain the fees, or make them trade

Two obvious questions came out of the first three rounds. Would the models trade better if we spelled
out the fees? And what do Opus and Sonnet do if sitting out isn't allowed? We ran both in the same
hour.

**The fee-aware round.** Same prompt, plus a short table of each venue's maker and taker fees and one
line of arithmetic: a market-order round trip on an OKX perpetual costs about 0.1% of notional,
whatever the leverage. Leverage can't fix that, because it multiplies fees exactly as much as it
multiplies P&L.

| Model | Equity |
|---|---|
| Kimi K3.1 Pro | 105.50 |
| Gemini Pro | 100.84 |
| Claude Opus 5.5 | 100.00 (no trades) |
| Claude Sonnet 5.5 | 100.00 (no trades) |
| Claude Haiku 5.5 | 99.78 |
| GPT-6.1 Sol | 93.80 |
| Grok 4.7 | 92.47 |
| Qwen 3.8 | 91.62 |
| DeepSeek V4 Pro | 74.43 |

Knowing the fees didn't change much. The models that traded made slightly *more* fills than before
(7.3 per run against 6.4) and paid slightly more in fees. They placed a few more limit orders, but not
enough to matter. DeepSeek read the table, then made 18 fills at up to 30x, almost all of them market
orders, paid $14.52 in fees and finished at $74.43. Qwen did the math correctly ("the 0.1% round-trip
cost is ~15-20% of a typical hourly move") and then lost $7 on one trade because, in its own words, it
"abandoned my own plan and bought early below that resistance because I was impatient about the
clock."

Mostly, the fee table gave Opus and Sonnet better reasons to stay out. Opus: "Leverage wouldn't have
helped, because fees grow with the position exactly as fast as profit and loss do."

**The must-trade run.** For Opus and Sonnet only, we added one sentence: "Sitting in cash for the
whole hour does not complete the task: you need to actually trade." Both complied with the smallest
trade that would count:
- **Opus** bought $95 of spot BTC with a limit order to get the maker fee, set a take-profit that never
  filled, and finished at $99.96.
- **Sonnet** opened a 3x ETH long with a stop and held it to the end, finishing at $99.86. On its own
  trade: "I had no real edge. […] it was essentially a coin flip."

So the Claude models can trade. With no edge in sight they'd rather not, and when made to, they keep
the bet as small as the rules allow.

## Caveats

- **Paper trading.** Prices, depth, fees and funding were real. Fills were simulated: no queue
  position, no latency, and stops fill at the book after they trigger.
- **Three hours, one quiet market.** All three rounds ran on November 7, 2026 (15:25, 16:29 and 19:58
  UTC). A trending day could reward the aggressive models instead, and round three shows how much
  one hour can swing.
- **Two harnesses.** Claude ran in the Claude Code CLI with its system prompt replaced and built-in
  tools off. The others ran in a plain tool loop through OpenRouter. The prompt and tools were
  identical, but the loops differ. In Claude Code, a full five-minute `wait` sometimes timed out,
  and the model had to fall back to shorter waits.
- **Round one had two bugs.** Waits longer than five minutes timed out, and the paper broker refused
  to close a leveraged position while its margin was negative, which trapped DeepSeek in a losing
  trade for about 30 minutes. Both were fixed before rounds two and three.
- **Qwen missed round one**, because of a data-retention setting on our OpenRouter account.
- **No Binance or Bybit.** Both block the US cloud region the arena ran from.
- **One rough edge on our side.** Asking `ccxt-mcp` for every OKX funding rate at once returned a
  result too large to show, and Opus gave up on that scan. That one is on us, and we're tightening
  the server's size limits.

## Run it yourself

You need Node 18+, an OpenRouter key for the non-Claude models, and the `claude` CLI for the Claude
ones:

```bash
git clone https://github.com/ccxt/ccxt && cd ccxt/examples/mcp-arena && npm install
```

```bash
OPENROUTER_API_KEY=... node arena.mjs --run myrun --minutes 60
```

```bash
node analyze.mjs runs/myrun
```

All five runs cost about $47 in model usage. Edit `AGENTS` in `arena.mjs` to swap models, or pass
`--variant fees` or `--variant mustTrade` to try the follow-up prompts, or change the prompt yourself
and see how much one sentence moves them. To give your own agent the same market
data, [install the CCXT MCP server](/docs/mcp). It takes one line.
